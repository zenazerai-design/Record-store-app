Building the foundations for GWI’s next stage of enterprise growth

Connecting API usage to the commercial model

GWI was evolving from a traditional SaaS business towards an AI-powered, API-first platform. As the product offering expanded, the systems supporting enterprise customers needed to evolve too.

I led cross-functional discovery into GWI’s enterprise administration platform, designed GWI’s first customer-facing API/MCP usage dashboard, and contributed to wider billing discovery that helped shape the foundational work needed to support future consumption-based billing.

Role
Senior Product Designer · 2026
Team
Product, Engineering, Analytics
Collaboration
Customer Success, Product Experience, Revenue, Account Management and API/MCP teams

The business was changing faster than the platform

GWI’s ambition was to grow Enterprise ARR to £50m within 24 months by expanding its AI product offering.

As the business evolved towards an AI-powered, API-first platform, its enterprise capabilities needed to evolve alongside it. Supporting this growth meant rethinking how enterprise customers managed their accounts, accessed products and interacted with the platform.

I joined to help modernise User Management. Before proposing solutions, I needed to understand how the existing platform worked, where it was falling short, and what would be needed to support the business’s next stage of growth.

Finding the problems behind the interface

I led cross-functional discovery across five teams to understand how enterprise administration worked in practice. Through stakeholder mapping, workshops and interviews, I explored how teams managed customers, where manual processes had become embedded, and how the systems supporting enterprise accounts connected.

The research exposed several significant operational challenges.

  • £36m — Commercial misalignment

    Revenue associated with discrepancies between what had been sold in Salesforce and what had been provisioned in the admin system.

  • ~500 hours — Manual administration

    Time spent on manual administration and support over six months.

  • 24% — Repetitive support requests

    The share of Customer Success Intercom tickets relating to seat-change requests.

  • 80m API calls — Limited usage visibility

    API calls recorded in a single day. Customers lacked visibility into their usage, leaving internal teams to investigate queries.

  • 2,600 organisations — Organisational complexity

    2,600 organisations associated with one enterprise client, illustrating the complexity of managing enterprise structures.

These findings revealed that the challenge went beyond individual interfaces. Commercial agreements, organisational structures, access management and usage data needed to work together more reliably.

Building a shared understanding

I brought together perspectives from across the business to understand how enterprise administration worked in practice. Stakeholder mapping, workshops and interviews helped uncover the dependencies between teams, systems and customer workflows.

Stakeholder mapping

Who owned which workflows, who needed to be involved and who needed to be informed.

Proto-persona workshop

Lightweight profiles to give us direction on information to validate with users.

Interviews

13 interviews across RevOps, Enterprise CS, Product, Engineering, AI, and Legal.

Service mapping

How work moved between systems and owners.

Four opportunity areas emerged:

  1. 01

    Build an enterprise operating model:

    create an organisational structure that reflects how enterprise customers buy, manage and delegate access.

  2. 02

    Enable self-service administration:

    allow enterprise administrators to manage users without relying on Customer Success.

  3. 03

    Make API/MCP usage visible:

    give customers a way to understand and monitor their usage.

  4. 04

    Align access with contracts:

    connect customer access and permissions to the commercial relationships that govern them.

Modelling the system before designing the interface

As discovery progressed, it became clear that many of the challenges stemmed from the way the platform represented organisations, users, permissions and products.

Before designing new experiences, I used object-oriented UX techniques to map the key entities and their relationships, creating a shared language across teams.

This helped make the underlying complexity tangible: what belonged to an organisation, how access should be governed, and where the existing model fell short of enterprise requirements.

It also helped distinguish between problems that could be addressed through interface design and those that depended on changes to the underlying platform.

The model informed a longer-term vision for enterprise administration, including clearer organisational relationships, delegated access and more scalable governance.

Self-serve administration

Working with engineers, I explored how roles, permissions and access controls could support enterprise requirements within the platform’s constraints.

Before designing the interface, we agreed the underlying permission rules. These included protecting organisations by ensuring they retained an administrator, preventing administrators from removing or changing their own roles, supporting internal operations, and keeping administrator access separate from paid seats.

Design iterations from the narrower initiative, which did not launch to customers.

Making role changes explicit

The original role-management design did not make role changes sufficiently clear. The revision made those changes more explicit and legible.

Making removal consequences clear

The original user-removal design did not make the consequences of removing a user clear. The revision made those consequences clearer.

I translated these rules into user flows and explored how the experience should handle different states, decisions and edge cases.

The trade-off: immediate delivery vs foundational architecture

The constraint

WorkOS introduced constraints around the organisational hierarchy we needed to support. The business moved towards a narrower self-service administration initiative, Octopus U, rather than addressing the wider hierarchy problem immediately.

The risk

I raised the risk that improving self-service without resolving the underlying organisational hierarchy could leave important enterprise requirements unsupported. The interface could improve individual workflows, but it couldn’t resolve the missing relationships in the platform model.

The outcome

The business proceeded with the narrower initiative, which was ultimately scrapped. It did not launch to customers. The work helped expose the gap between the immediate delivery scope and the foundational capabilities the wider enterprise platform needed.

Giving enterprise customers visibility into API/MCP usage

As GWI expanded its API and MCP offering, customer-facing usage visibility became an important part of the wider platform experience.

The research had exposed a practical problem: customers couldn’t easily see how they were using the APIs, and internal teams had to investigate usage queries. The dashboard needed to give customers clearer information without making them interpret unreliable or confusing data.

I designed GWI’s first customer-facing API/MCP usage dashboard around four core customer questions:

  • Are we approaching our limits?
  • What caused that spike?
  • How is usage trending?
  • Who is consuming the shared allocation?

Turning customer questions into trustworthy reporting

Three design decisions shaped the reporting experience.

1. Separate API from MCP

The design separates API and MCP usage so customers can understand distinct consumption patterns.

2. Show meaningful usage

The reporting prioritises meaningful usage information rather than simply showing requested data.

3. Flexibility without a filter wall

The design provides flexibility without overwhelming customers with a wall of filters.

Bridging design and implementation

A further challenge was finding a charting pattern that worked across Figma and React.

I worked through the product use case, component design and implementation requirements to agree the chart behaviour, states and flexibility. This helped connect the design intent to a pattern that could be implemented consistently.

The dashboard has been technically delivered but is undergoing usage-data QA before customer release. Customer adoption and commercial impact have not yet been established.

Connecting usage to the commercial model

Alongside the dashboard work, I contributed to cross-functional discovery into API/MCP billing.

Usage, products, organisations and commercial agreements need to connect across the platform to support future consumption-based billing. Customer-facing usage visibility was only one part of that challenge.

I advocated for foundational Salesforce mapping and organisational hierarchy work before a broader consumption-based billing rollout. That work is now underway.

Outcomes and impact

Delivered

Designed and built GWI’s first customer-facing API/MCP usage dashboard, now undergoing data QA before customer release.

Discovered

Identified £36m in revenue misalignment and around 500 hours of manual administration and support over six months.

Influenced

Helped shape priorities around organisational hierarchy, Salesforce mapping and the foundations for consumption-based billing.

Next steps

Foundational platform work is underway. Customer adoption and commercial impact are yet to be established.

Reflection: Designing systems, not just interfaces

This work reinforced how closely enterprise UX is tied to the systems and commercial relationships behind it. Designing a better interface can improve a workflow, but it can’t resolve missing relationships between organisations, products, access and usage.

My role was to make those dependencies visible, deliver a useful customer-facing capability, and help the business understand which foundational problems needed to be addressed next.

The dashboard was a tangible delivery, while the wider discovery helped clarify the operational and commercial foundations needed for GWI’s enterprise platform to scale.
